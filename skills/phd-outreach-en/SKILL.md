# PhD Outreach

Make the email a short preview of research experience: show who the applicant is, what they personally did and when they can start, giving the professor a reason to read the CV or continue the conversation. Do not set an immediate offer as the email's goal.

Treat the method as one professor's experience, mainly for initial contact in US PhD applications. Adapt it to the region, discipline and recruitment instructions. Do not attribute it to an official university policy. For provenance or disputed advice, read the [source notes](references/source-notes.md).

## Extract the facts first

1. Read the supplied draft, CV, research notes and professor requirements. Reuse known information. If only a draft is supplied, revise it without requiring a CV.
2. Identify the name, degree and field, institution and country where useful, expected graduation, intended PhD start term, recipient's surname and research area. Keep graduation and start dates separate. Include GPA only when supplied, the scale is clear and it adds useful evidence.
3. Select one convincing relevant project and one or two actual contributions. Add a second project only if it adds information. Use “research question → personal action and method → known result or demonstrated capability.” Do not invent a result when none is supplied.
4. Distinguish personal tasks from team achievements, and published, accepted, under-review, preprint and unpublished work. Do not turn participation into leadership, submission into publication or exploratory findings into validated conclusions. Applicants without publications can describe actual research work without apologising.
5. Follow the supplied requirements. If a fact needs verification, or the user requests research, consult official pages, recruitment notices or original papers for facts that affect the email. Do not infer current projects, funding or openings from older papers.
6. Produce a draft with clear placeholders such as [Expected graduation] and [Intended start term] for missing information, then list essential gaps outside the email. Do not silently resolve conflicting facts or turn remembered personal details into current application facts without confirmation.

## Default to three short paragraphs

Aim for roughly 150–200 English words, with three short paragraphs. This is a practical reference, not a word limit from the professor. Use fewer words when appropriate, and follow the user's language, length and format preferences. Explain edits in the user's language.

- **Subject and salutation:** use “PhD inquiry — [Intended start term] — [Research area]”; omit or mark unknown components. For a known professor, use “Dear Professor [Surname],” unless a specified title takes priority. Avoid stacking titles.
- **Paragraph 1, background and timing:** state the current degree, institution, expected graduation and purpose directly. GPA is optional. A routine greeting can be omitted.
- **Paragraph 2, contributions:** use two to four sentences for one concrete question and one or two personal contributions. Prefer specific actions and methods to unsupported self-assessments such as passionate, hard-working or extensive experience. Mention publication status briefly only when useful; do not lead with paper counts.
- **Paragraph 3, next step:** make the intended start term clear, ask whether the professor is considering students for that intake and offer a short conversation. Write “I have attached my CV” only when the user confirms that arrangement. Sign with the applicant's name.

Remove long motivations, life stories, repeated descriptions of the professor's work and generic praise. Retain at most one supported connection between the applicant's actual experience and a relevant research question when it helps assessment. Do not claim a perfect fit or force in paper titles or an unsolicited full proposal.

A CV is the default attachment plan; explicit recruitment requirements take priority. Keep detailed projects, papers and coursework in the CV. A referral request is optional when the user wants a wider search, not a fixed closing sentence.

For a drafting skeleton, read the [email template](assets/email-template.txt) and adapt it rather than filling every slot mechanically. When the user wants a portable prompt, provide the [standalone prompt](assets/reusable-prompt.txt).

## Follow-ups

Use this mode only when requested. Read the original email, sending date, replies and recipient instructions.

- After about a week without a reply, a short reply in the same thread may be appropriate. Follow any automatic reply, recruitment schedule or instruction not to send inquiry emails.
- Draft only the follow-up currently needed, roughly 40–70 words: a reminder of the earlier message, the intended start term or one useful update and a short request. Do not paste the full original email again.
- The source professor regarded up to three attempts as acceptable. Treat that as an individual preference, not a target. Avoid repeated pressure and stop after an explicit refusal or request to stop contact.
- Do not equate no reply with lack of ability or promise a response after following up.

## Deliver and check

Provide one subject line and one copyable email, followed by up to three brief notes on important changes. List essential missing or unverified facts separately. If the user requests only the email, omit commentary while keeping placeholders visible.

Before delivery, check:

- Can the reader quickly find the identity, graduation date, intended start term, personal contributions and next step?
- Is there at least one concrete task, with no invented results, GPA, authorship, publication status or recruitment information?
- Have repetition, unsupported self-assessments, excessive praise and claims of personal credit for team work been removed?
- Does the draft agree with the supplied CV and actual attachment arrangement?
- Does it follow explicit recipient instructions and mark all unknowns?

Writing an email does not itself include sending it or scheduling reminders. Perform those actions only on a separate explicit request.
